Load Range E Does Not Mean Ten Plies, and the Listings Prove It

Five all-terrain tires in one size, 285/70-17, priced within twenty dollars of each other — and the load labels on them do not mean what the internet says they mean. The most heavily rated tire in the group has the fewest sidewall plies. The one with the mildest label carries the second-highest load

By Duffy Vance, Editor Published 2026-11-19
The short answer

Of five 285/70-17 tires, the Load Range E model lists 10 tread plies and only 3 sidewall plies. The Load Range C model lists 6 and 6. Ply rating was retired as a standard decades ago and survives as a marketing number, so the two fields describe different things — and the sidewall count is the one that is not the old ply system at all.

The detail worth repeating

The P-metric Toyo lists a 2,833 lb. max load. The LT Load Range C BFGoodrich lists 2,755 lb. The tire with the heavier badge carries 78 lb. less, costs the same $320.99, and weighs 11 lb. more.

Five all-terrain tires in one size, 285/70-17, priced within twenty dollars of each other — and the load labels on them do not mean what the internet says they mean. The most heavily rated tire in the group has the fewest sidewall plies. The one with the mildest label carries the second-highest load.

Load range and ply rating get used interchangeably in forum posts and product titles. They are not the same system, the conversion tables that circulate are not reliable, and you can watch both break down in the published specs.

Every figure below was read from the product spec block on 19 November 2026. All five are the same size and were read the same day, so the comparison is like for like.

Five tires, and the fields that disagree

Tire Price Load range Load index Max load Tread plies Sidewall plies Weight
Toyo Open Country A/T III $320.99 P-metric (none listed) 117 2,833 lbs. not published not published 46.0 lbs.
BFGoodrich All-Terrain T/A KO3 $320.99 C 116/113 2,755 lbs. 6 6 57.0 lbs.
Falken Wildpeak A/T4W $316.99 E 126/123 3,750 lbs. 10 3 65.4 lbs.
Mickey Thompson Baja Boss A/T $307.99 SL 116 2,756 lbs. not published not published 48.8 lbs.
Nitto Ridge Grappler $300.99 XL 116 2,756 lbs. not published not published 48.7 lbs.

The Falken is the only genuinely heavy-duty tire in this group and it is easy to identify: 3,750 lbs. against roughly 2,755 for everything else. That number, not the letter, is the thing you are buying.

What ply rating used to mean, and why the letter survived

Ply rating was a real count once — the number of cotton cord plies in the carcass. When materials changed, the count stopped describing anything physical and the industry kept the letters as a shorthand for a load band. So “Load Range E” is a capacity class, not an instruction to count anything.

That is why the labels and the ply fields diverge, and the Falken shows it most clearly:

  • 10 tread plies.
  • 3 sidewall plies.

A tire marketed under a rating that people read as “10-ply” publishes a sidewall count of three. That is not a contradiction or a trick — tread ply quantity and sidewall ply quantity are separate construction details, and neither one is the ply rating. The rating is a load band. The plies are how that particular tire is built.

The BFGoodrich goes the other way: 6 and 6, an even count, on a Load Range C tire. If you were converting letters to plies you would expect the C to be the low one. On sidewall construction it is the higher of the two.

The forty-dollar spread buys almost nothing except at the top

Four of these five tires sit between $300.99 and $320.99 — a $20 window. Over that window the published max load barely moves:

  • Nitto, $300.99 — 2,756 lbs.
  • Mickey Thompson, $307.99 — 2,756 lbs.
  • Falken, $316.99 — 3,750 lbs.
  • BFGoodrich, $320.99 — 2,755 lbs.
  • Toyo, $320.99 — 2,833 lbs.

Four tires, four different load range letters — SL, XL, C, and none at all — and the load figure lands within 78 lbs. of itself across all four. The letter is not a price tier and it is not a capacity tier at this size. Only the Falken separates, and it separates on the number, not the letter.

The P-metric tire that out-carries the LT

This is the one worth sitting with. The Toyo is a P-metric — a passenger-car construction, listed without a load range letter at all. The BFGoodrich is an LT Load Range C, the light-truck construction the internet tells you to buy for a truck.

  • Toyo P-metric: 2,833 lbs., $320.99, 46.0 lbs.
  • BFGoodrich LT Load Range C: 2,755 lbs., $320.99, 57.0 lbs.

The P-metric carries 78 lbs. more, costs exactly the same, and is 11 lbs. lighter per corner. Forty-four pounds of unsprung and rotating mass across four tires, in exchange for giving up 78 lbs. of rated capacity you were not using.

That is not an argument that P-metric is always right — LT construction exists for reasons beyond the load number, and this article is not going to invent durability claims it cannot source. It is an argument that the LT badge is not self-justifying. On these two listings, the badge costs 11 lbs. a corner and 78 lbs. of capacity.

Why the lightest tire is not the weakest

Weight tracks construction, not capacity. Sort the five by weight:

  • Toyo — 46.0 lbs.
  • Nitto — 48.7 lbs.
  • Mickey Thompson — 48.8 lbs.
  • BFGoodrich — 57.0 lbs.
  • Falken — 65.4 lbs.

The Falken is heaviest and strongest, so weight is doing something. But the Toyo is the lightest and the second-strongest, while the BFGoodrich is second-heaviest and carries the least of the five. Weight bought construction in some of these and not in others, and only the Falken’s weight clearly buys capacity.

The fields that are simply missing

Of five listings, only two publish ply quantities at all — the BFGoodrich and the Falken. Three of five leave both ply fields off entirely.

That matters because the ply number is the one people go looking for. If you want to know how a tire is built rather than what band it is filed under, three of these five pages give you no way to find out. A missing field is not a zero, and it is not evidence of anything — it is just not published.

Reading a tire listing in the right order

  1. Max load in lbs. The only field that means what it appears to mean.
  2. Load index. The number the letter is standing in for.
  3. Load range letter. A band, useful for comparing within a brand.
  4. Tread and sidewall ply quantity if published — and if not, notice that.
  5. Weight per tire. Four corners, so multiply by four before dismissing it.
  6. Speed rating and 3PMSF, which affect use far more than the letter.
  7. Price, last.

What the vehicle decides, not the tire

A load range cannot raise the weight your truck is allowed to carry. That number is set by the vehicle — axle ratings, springs, brakes and the door jamb label — and a Load Range E tire bolted to a truck rated for 1,500 lbs. of payload does not make it a 3,000 lb. payload truck. It makes it a truck with stiff tires.

The reverse also holds: fitting a tire rated well above your axle’s capacity buys you margin and nothing else. It does not buy you legal capacity.

Does any of this touch CARB?

No. Tires are not emissions equipment, there is no Executive Order to check, and load range has no bearing on a smog inspection. Tire and wheel fitment is a separate question governed by vehicle equipment rules, and this site’s CARB checks — which apply to intakes, exhausts and tuners — do not reach it.

What I could not verify

  • Load capacity at pressure. Max load is quoted at a stated inflation pressure, and the pressure was not read for all five. Capacity at 35 psi is not the figure in the table.
  • Ply quantities for three of the five. Not published on those pages, so not quoted. The absence is reported, not filled in.
  • Durability, puncture resistance or tread life beyond the published UTQG and warranty text. No test data was available to me for any of these.
  • Whether any of these is the correct tire for your vehicle. Load index needs to meet or exceed the vehicle’s requirement, which comes off the door jamb, not off a comparison table.
