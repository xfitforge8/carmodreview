GM's Lifter Problem: Which Trucks It Hits and What the Bulletins Say

“If it’s a 5.3, are the lifters going to fail?” is the first question worth asking when you look at a used Silverado or Sierra. It is also the question most listings will not answer for you, and the one a pre-purchase inspection is least likely to catch.

The good news is that GM has written it down. Its own service bulletins name specific model years, engine codes and build dates. The bad news is that the paperwork does not mean what most people assume it means — it lists vehicles worth checking, not vehicles guaranteed to fail.

The single most useful thing a buyer can do is stop shopping by model year and start shopping by engine code and build date. Here is why.

AFM and DFM are not the same system

GM has used two different cylinder-deactivation systems on the 5.3, and they do not share documentation.

Active Fuel Management (AFM) shut off a fixed set of cylinders under light load. It is on the older engines and on some newer ones.

Dynamic Fuel Management (DFM) arrived for 2019 on the 5.3L L84 and the 6.2L L87, and can run on more combinations of active cylinders.

The two systems use different lifters and different bulletins. More importantly, the engine code and the system do not line up the way people assume. The L82 uses AFM. The L84 uses DFM. So “my truck is a 2021, so it must be DFM” is not a safe conclusion, and neither is its reverse. This is the first place a buyer goes wrong.

What the bulletins actually list

These are GM documents, with their own applicability ranges. None of them is a failure-rate study, and none of them says every engine in the range will fail.

Bulletin Covers What it describes
PIP4568S (March 2016) AFM-equipped 2007–2013 Silverado and Sierra; 2007–2014 Tahoe, Suburban, Yukon Misfires and ticking from AFM lifters that collapse or stick, including locking-pin concerns
15-06-01-002O (revised March 2023) 5.3L L83 and L8B: 2014–2018 Silverado and Sierra, carryover 2019 Silverado LD and Sierra Limited, 2015–2020 full-size SUVs Collapsed or stuck AFM lifters, bent pushrods, possible locking-pin damage from oil aeration
23-NA-043 (revised March 2024) 5.3L L84 in specified 2020–2021 Silverado and Sierra 1500, and 2021 Tahoe/Suburban/Yukon, built 1 September 2020 – 31 March 2021 Bent pushrod or collapsed/separated lifter with misfires
PIP5861 (21 July 2022) 5.3L L84 in certain 2022 Silverado and Sierra 1500 and SUVs, built 9 October – 18 December 2021 Internal lifter concern leading to a collapsed or separated lifter and/or bent pushrod
19-NA-218 (June 2024 revision) Applications through model year 2024 Several potential causes of misfire, ticking and related concerns

Two things in that table matter more than the rest.

The 2020–2022 windows are build dates, not model years. A 2021 Silverado built in July 2021 falls outside both. A 2020 built in October 2020 falls inside the first one. The door-jamb sticker tells you the build date, and that is the date to check against the bulletin — not the year on the title.

Both later bulletins also contain 6.2L applications. Those should not be confused with the 5.3L ranges, and neither should be conflated with the separate 6.2L crankshaft and bearing recall, which is a different problem with different paperwork.

The two windows worth knowing if you are buying an L84

If you are shopping a 2020–2022 Silverado or Sierra with the 5.3L L84, there are exactly two production windows GM documented:

  • 1 September 2020 – 31 March 2021
  • 9 October – 18 December 2021

Both involve the same failure: a lifter that collapses or separates, and a pushrod that bends with it. The symptoms in both documents are misfires and the related rough running that follows.

Outside those windows, GM’s guidance still points technicians to diagnostic material — it does not say the engine is fine. That distinction is the whole problem with buying on model year alone, and it is why the two ranges in the table are worth more than any list of “years to avoid.”

What GM says it changed

In an interview with Getty’s Garage, GM assistant chief engineer Ian Stahl attributed many of the earlier failures to supplier quality, including poorly formed wire, and described supplier changes and a revised spring inside the deactivation mechanism. He placed those changes at roughly four years before the interview and said warranty activity had been low since.

“It’s a problem that’s behind us, I think,” he said.

Two limits on that quote. He did not supply claim totals, failure rates or an exact production cutoff. And the interview was about the new 2027 V8 generation — the improvements Stahl describes came before those trucks, with the DFM system continuing. It would be a misreading to take it as confirmation that a new lifter system arrives for 2027. It is GM’s assessment that the problem is resolved, not proof that any engine built after a particular date is trouble-free.

The lawsuit is separate from the bulletins, and slower

A class action filed in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Michigan in December 2021 alleges that lifters and valvetrain parts in a range of GM trucks and SUVs — Silverado, Sierra and Cadillac Escalade among them — are prone to premature failure. The plaintiffs name the 5.3L L84 and 6.2L L87 specifically, alleging misfires, ticking and stalling from lightweight lifters installed in the wrong positions, and argue that replacement parts carry the same defect.

In March 2026 the plaintiffs tried to split the case into separate AFM and DFM actions, arguing they had found a significant distinction between the two. Judge Laurie J. Michelson denied the motion, noting that the court had already identified three separate part failures at issue back in January 2023 and that the request came “four years after the suit was filed and almost three years since the Court issued that warning.”

The case has been consolidated since December 2021 — over four years — with arbitration claims sent out and returning. No recall and no payout exist as of this writing. If you are waiting for a settlement to cover a repair, do not budget around it.

What to actually do

If you are buying. Get the VIN before you go see the truck. Ask a Chevy or GMC dealer to confirm the engine code and the exact build date. If it is an L84, check the build date against 23-NA-043 and PIP5861. If the seller says the lifters were replaced, ask for the repair order — it should show which parts were replaced and whether the shop worked on one bank or both.

Then hear it start cold. A persistent tick, a rough idle or a misfire code is worth diagnosing before you buy, not after.

If it is your truck. A tick or a misfire code does not by itself identify a failed lifter, and several other things cause both. Ask the shop to diagnose it before authorizing a lifter repair, and bring the bulletin number if your build date matches one.

If you are buying new. The 2027 V8 generation replaces the 5.3 with a 5.7 and continues DFM. GM’s position is that the supplier fixes are already in. There is no independent failure-rate data for those engines yet, in either direction.

Why this is worth more than a list of years

The popular version of this article is “avoid the 5.3 from these years.” That is easier to read and worse to shop with, because it is wrong in both directions: it condemns thousands of engines built outside the documented windows, and it reassures buyers of an L84 built inside one.

The bulletins are specific because GM had to be. Match the engine code, then the build date, then decide.
