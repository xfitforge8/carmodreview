Best Cold Air Intakes for a 5.0L F-150, Ranked Three Ways

We pulled 14 listings for the Coyote V8, cross-checked owner ratings against price, and separated the intakes that are actually CARB-legal from the ones whose listings stay quiet about it. Three picks: cheapest that isn't junk, best looking, and the one to buy if you only want to think about it once.

Published 30 October 2026 14 products compared

Cold air intakes are the most oversold part in the truck aftermarket. The listings promise horsepower, the forums argue about whether that horsepower is real, and almost nobody tells you the boring thing that actually determines whether you'll be happy: does it fit your exact truck, and is it legal where you live.

So that's what this is organised around. We looked at price, at what owners actually rated the thing after living with it, and at whether the listing states CARB exemption — because in California, and in a handful of other states that follow its rules, an intake without it is a failed inspection waiting to happen.

The short answer

If you have a 2018–2026 5.0L F-150 and want to stop reading here:

  • Best all-round: Roush, around $430. CARB-legal, 4.7 stars over 500+ ratings, and it's the one with the longest track record on this platform.
  • Best value: SR Performance, around $150. A third of the price and rated slightly higher. You give up brand resale and some fit-and-finish polish.
  • Best design: aFe Momentum GT, around $435. Sealed housing, dry filter, cleanest engine bay of the group.

Keep reading if you want the reasoning, or if you have an earlier truck — fitment changes by year and the picks shift with it.

The three picks

Best all-round

Roush Cold Air Intake (18–26 5.0L F-150)

$429.99 · 4.7 stars from 500+ ratings

Roush is the safe pick, and "safe" is doing real work in that sentence. It's the intake with the deepest review base on this generation of F-150, which means the failure modes are already documented by other people rather than discovered by you. The listing states CARB exemption, which most of the cheaper options don't bother to.

The honest downside: you are paying roughly $280 more than the SR Performance for a similar power outcome. What that money buys is fitment, a known quantity, and a badge that holds some resale value. If none of those matter to you, don't pay it.

Best value

SR Performance Cold Air Intake (15–20 5.0L F-150)

$149.99 · 4.6 stars

This is the pick for anyone who wants the intake for the intake's sake and doesn't care about the name on the tube. At $150 it's less than a third of the Roush, and its owner rating is marginally higher, which tells you the price gap isn't buying reliability.

What it does buy is fitment tolerance and finish. Cheap intakes are where you find the reviews complaining about a coupler that needed trimming or a heat shield that kissed the hood liner. Read the recent reviews before you commit rather than trusting the average alone.

Note the year range: this one covers 2015–2020. If you have a 2021 or newer, it does not fit — that's the generation split, and it's the single most common ordering mistake on this part.

Best design

aFe Momentum GT Cold Air Intake, Pro DRY S (21–26 5.0L F-150)

$435.00 · 4.8 stars

The Momentum GT is a sealed-box design rather than an open cone, which is the engineering-correct answer to the thing open intakes get wrong: they pull hot air from the engine bay at idle. Whether that matters on a truck doing highway miles is debatable. Whether it looks right is not — this is the cleanest engine bay of the three, and the dry filter means no oiling ritual at service time.

The dry filter is the underrated part. Oiled filters, done wrong, can contaminate a mass airflow sensor and give you a rough idle you'll spend a weekend chasing. Dry media removes that whole category of self-inflicted problem.

The horsepower question, answered honestly

You will see "up to +15 hp" on intake listings. Here is what we could and could not establish.

What's well documented across the independent testing we reviewed: an intake on a modern, factory-tuned truck typically produces a gain in the low single digits at the wheels, and the gain tends to be larger at high RPM than where you actually drive. What is reliably real is the sound — intake noise under load changes noticeably, and that's what most buyers are actually chasing.

What we could not verify: we found no dyno sheet published alongside these specific listings, and we did not run one. So we are not going to print a horsepower number and dress it up as our own finding. Anyone who does, on a page like this, is guessing.

Buy it for the right reason

If a few horsepower is the goal, an intake is a poor value — a tuner is where the measurable change lives, and it costs more but does more. If induction sound and engine bay appearance are the goal, an intake delivers immediately and this page is the right place to be. Both are legitimate. Just don't buy one expecting a transformation it won't provide.

Three things to check before you order

  1. Year, exactly. The 2015–2020 and 2021–2026 trucks take different parts. Confirm from the sticker in the driver's door jamb.
  2. Engine, exactly. The 5.0L V8, 2.7L EcoBoost, and 3.5L EcoBoost take different intakes. Several products in the table above look identical and are not interchangeable.
  3. CARB status if you're in California. Only the Roush listing we checked states exemption outright. If inspection matters where you live, that single fact outweighs every other consideration on this page.

What we couldn't verify

We did not install these intakes, and we have no long-term test data. Ratings and prices are as the listings showed them on 30 October 2026. We found no published dyno results for these specific SKUs, so we've made no horsepower claim of our own. CARB status for several products is listed as "not stated" rather than "not legal" — absence of a statement is not a denial, but confirm before relying on it.
