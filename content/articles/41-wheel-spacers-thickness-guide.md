# Wheel Spacers: Thickness Does Not Predict Price, and Bore Decides Fit

## EN

**Shopping wheel spacers by thickness will lead you to the wrong part, because
thickness is the one spec that barely moves the price.** On the same shelf, a
quarter-inch spacer runs $13.99 and another quarter-inch spacer runs $70.01. A
two-inch bolt-on with studs costs $89.99, while a 1.25-inch bolt-on costs
$159.99. Nothing about the price ladder follows the thickness ladder.

The demand behind this question is enormous and nearly all of it is one worry:
the safety videos on this topic run **2 million, 1.9 million, 1.7 million, 1.5
million and 752,000 views**. Nine spacers are broken down below, every figure
read on 18 November 2026.

### The number that decides fit, and it is not thickness

**Center bore.** This is the diameter of the hole in the middle of the spacer,
and it has to match the hub it sits on. Two spacers can share a price, a
thickness and a bolt pattern and still not be interchangeable, because the hole
in the middle is a different size.

Here is the proof from this exact shelf. The **Spidertrax 1.25-inch spacer has a
center bore of 106.17 mm.** The **Trail-Gear 1.25-inch spacer has a center bore
of 72.00 mm.** Both are $159.99. Both are 1.250 inch thick. Both are sold as a
pair. One is built for a hub that is 34 mm larger in diameter than the other, and
neither will fit the other's truck.

That is 34 mm — well over an inch — of difference that no product title, no price
and no thickness figure reveals.

### Which type you actually need

**A slip-on spacer** is a flat disc with no studs. Your original wheel studs pass
through it and through the wheel, and the lugnuts clamp the whole stack. Every
millimetre of it consumes the same amount of thread.

**A bolt-on spacer** — properly an adapter — bolts to the hub using the original
studs and carries its own studs for the wheel. It has to be an inch or more thick
because it holds two sets of fasteners inside its own body.

The spec table tells you which one you are looking at, in a line most buyers skip:
**Wheel Studs Included: Yes or No.** Three of the spacers below say No and five
say Yes. That single field is the real dividing line on this shelf, not the price.

### The $70 quarter-inch spacer and the $14 quarter-inch spacer

Set them side by side and the price gap looks absurd. Both are 0.250 inch thick.
Both are sold as a pair. Both have no studs. The Summit is **$13.99** and the Baer
is **$70.01**.

The difference is how they are made. Baer's listing says **billet aluminum** —
machined from a solid block. The Summit part makes a billet claim at a different
thickness but not at this one. A quarter inch of machined billet costs more to
produce than a quarter inch of stamped material, and on a part that carries the
entire clamp load of a corner of the vehicle, tolerance on a flat face matters.

Then look at the reviews. The Baer has **46**, the most of any spacer here, and
the Summit has **3**. Buyers on this shelf are not shopping on price alone.

### The two-inch bolt-on that costs less than the 1.25-inch one

This is the hardest fact on the page to get your head around. The Rough Country
1101 is **2.000 inches thick, includes wheel studs, and costs $89.99.** The
Spidertrax WHS007K is **1.250 inches thick, includes wheel studs, and costs
$159.99.**

The thicker part with the same feature set is **$70 cheaper**. If you assumed the
price ladder tracked the thickness ladder, this is where that assumption breaks.

Rough Country's own copy explains what it is for: *"Designed to allow the use of
wider tires for a beefier stance."* Note what it does not claim — no billet
machining — and its listing carries **1 review**. The Spidertrax part is described
as billet aluminum and publishes a center bore of 106.17 mm. You are paying for
the machining spec, not the inches.

That same Rough Country listing also carries a caution worth reading before you
assume hub-centric: *"Some models feature a hub-centric design (application
dependent)."* Hub-centric means the wheel centres on the hub bore rather than on
the studs. On this part it is not guaranteed by the part number — it is guaranteed
by the specific application.

### The quarter-inch spacer that is not a stance part at all

The cheapest Rough Country quarter-inch, the 1065 at **$39.99**, comes with a line
in its feature list that changes what the part is for:

*"Designed to allow the use of factory wheels with Rough Country lift kits."*

That is a clearance shim. It exists so a factory wheel stops contacting a control
arm or a brake caliper after a lift kit moves the suspension. It is not there to
push wheels outward for looks, and its 0.250 inch of thickness would be almost
invisible if you bought it for that.

The 1101 at $89.99 is the one that says "beefier stance." **Two Rough Country
spacers, a $50 gap, and the cheaper one is a different job entirely.**

### Watch the quantity line

The Summit SUM-75832B is **$29.99 and sold as Each**, not as a pair. Every other
spacer in the table is a pair. Bought for one axle, that is $59.98 — which changes
the comparison against the Trans-Dapt pair at $150.99 entirely.

The quantity field is the second most-skipped line after the studs field, and it
is the one that turns a $29.99 bargain into a $59.98 purchase.

### What actually fails, and why

**Too little thread engagement.** The most common real failure. A slip-on spacer
consumes stud thread in exact proportion to its thickness. A common working rule
is eight to nine full turns of nut on an M12 or M14 stud. If a quarter inch of
spacer costs you that, you need longer studs, which is a hub-off job on many
trucks.

**Wrong center bore.** A spacer whose bore is larger than the hub does not centre
the wheel. The wheel then locates on the studs alone, and the symptom is a
vibration that comes and goes with road speed.

**The inner fastener set.** A bolt-on spacer has two sets of nuts — spacer to hub,
and wheel to spacer. The inner set cannot be seen with the wheel fitted, and it is
the set that gets forgotten. Recheck both after the first drive.

**Deformation under clamp load.** A spacer that deforms loses torque. This is the
real argument for billet at $70 over stamped at $13.99 on a part this thin.

### The order to do this in

**1. Find your hub bore**, not just your bolt pattern. The bolt pattern tells you
the studs line up. The bore tells you whether the wheel centres. This is the spec
that separates the two $159.99 spacers above.

**2. Decide slip-on or bolt-on** by reading the studs field. Under half an inch
with long factory studs, a slip-on can work. Over half an inch, you want the
bolt-on that carries its own studs.

**3. Measure thread engagement** if you are going slip-on. Eight to nine turns
minimum.

**4. Check the quantity.** Each versus pair is a 2x swing and it is one line in the
spec table.

**5. Recheck torque after the first drive**, both fastener sets on a bolt-on.

### Is any of this legal in California?

Wheel spacers are not emissions equipment. There is no CARB Executive Order
question, no EO number to look for, and nothing here affects a smog inspection.

What does apply is the vehicle code on tire coverage. California requires tires to
be covered by the fenders, and moving wheels outboard can put the tread outside
the fender line. That is a fix-it ticket rather than an emissions one, and it is
worth checking the coverage after fitting rather than assuming a small change
stays invisible.
