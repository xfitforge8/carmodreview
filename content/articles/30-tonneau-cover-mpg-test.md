# Do Tonneau Covers Improve MPG? Three Tests, Three Answers

## EN

**The same question has been tested on video at least three times, and the three
answers do not agree.** A 2022 Ram 1500 gained 1.6 mpg with a cover on. A 2022 Ford
Maverick came out 5% better on its trip computer and exactly level at the fuel pump.
A 2026 Toyota Tacoma went up, then back down, across three back-to-back runs.

That spread is the honest answer, and it is more useful than either "covers save
gas" or "covers are a myth." All three tests were run in good faith, and the difference
between them tells you what actually decides the outcome on your truck.

### What each test actually did

| Test | Truck | Route | Cover on | Cover off | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TFL, Dec 2022 | 2022 Ram 1500, 3.6L V6 | ~57 mi, 65 then 75 mph | 21.1 mpg | 19.5 mpg | **+1.6** |
| Daily Motor | 2022 Maverick, 2.0L EcoBoost | 60 mi, ~70 mph | 34.4 mpg* | 32.6 mpg* | **+5%** |
| Daily Motor | 2026 Tacoma TRD OR Hybrid | 3 × 50 mi, 71 mph | 22.5, 23.0 | 23.5 | **~0** |

\*The Maverick's two figures are the truck's own trip computer. At the pump the two
runs used 1.862 and 1.863 gallons — the same number twice.

Every one of those runs used the same route in both directions, topped off at the
same pump, and held speed with cruise control set against GPS rather than the
speedometer. Those are the right controls, and it is why the numbers are worth
comparing at all.

### The Ram test: the cleanest +1.6 mpg

The Fast Lane Truck ran a 2022 Ram 1500 Tradesman on a highway loop, roughly 57 miles
by GPS, at the 65 and 75 mph speed limits, sitting around 2,200 rpm and dropping to
1,900 in eighth gear. The truck is the 3.6-liter Pentastar V6, 305 hp, eight-speed
automatic, EPA rated 16 city and 23 highway.

With a soft Mopar tonneau clamped on: **57.0 miles on 2.696 gallons, 21.1 mpg.** With
the cover off: **57 miles on 2.918 gallons, 19.5 mpg.** That is the 1.6 mpg gain.

Two details make this the most trustworthy of the three. When they took the cover off
they **left it in the bed**, so the 25–30 lb cover was present in both runs and the
comparison isolates the shape rather than the weight. And they calculated from the pump
every time instead of trusting the dash — with good reason, because the truck's own trip
meter claimed 21.9 mpg on the no-cover run against a pump figure of 19.5. That is the
same trip-meter miss that turned up in the Maverick test below.

### The Maverick test: the pump said no change

Daily Motor ran a 60-mile loop on a 2022 Maverick XLT with the 2.0-liter EcoBoost,
FX4 and 4K tow packages, about 10,000 miles on it, and a Truxedo Sentry CT hard
roll-up. Cruise set at a GPS 72, tire pressures at the door placard of 35 psi cold,
climate at 73 on auto, 48°F ambient.

With the cover rolled open: **60.8 miles on 1.862 gallons, 32.6 mpg.** With it closed
and latched: **60.8 miles on 1.863 gallons, 32.6 mpg.** One thousandth of a gallon
apart.

The truck's trip computer disagreed, reading 32.6 for the open run and 34.4 for the
closed one — a 1.8 mpg gap, which works out to 5.5%, which the host rounded down to
5%. He went with the computer, said plainly that he was skeptical of two fills landing
that close, and flagged that the pump had more margin for error in it than he would
like.

Both readings are in the video, and the 5% figure is the one that circulates. It is
derived from the dash display, not from the fuel that went into the tank. That does
not make it wrong. It makes it a five percent difference the pump could not see.

### The Tacoma test: the answer flipped twice

The same channel then ran a 2026 Tacoma TRD Off-Road with the i-Force Max hybrid,
using a BAK Revolver X4S, and did something smarter than a single A-to-B: three
consecutive 50-mile legs, closed, open, closed again, to catch exactly the kind of
drift that spoils a two-run test.

The result was **22.5 mpg closed, 23.5 open, 23.0 closed.** The middle run beat both
runs where the cover was down.

The host was upfront about why he did not trust that ordering. The third leg hit
construction, dropped to a 65.1 mph average against 67.1, and caught two short
downpours. He also noted that the owner had removed the front air dam, and that the
short bed and short cab may leave less wake for a cover to tidy up than a long bed
does. His conclusion was that a tonneau made no appreciable difference on this truck,
and that he would not spend on one purely hoping for fuel economy.

### What the wind tunnel says, and what it does not

There is a real primary source here, and it is older and narrower than the blog posts
suggest. **SAE paper 881874**, "Pickup Truck Drag Reduction — Devices That Reduce Drag
Without Limiting Truck Utility," by Maxwell, Jones and Jones at Texas Tech University,
was published for the SAE International Truck and Bus Meeting and Exposition on
**7 November 1988**.

Two devices were tested: a wing structure behind the top rear of the cab, and a cover
over the rear portion of the bed. Per the abstract, **both demonstrated 5% to 6% drag
reductions**, and the bed cover alone **reduced vehicle lift by 30%**. Together, in
scale-model testing, they produced drag reduction on the order of **17%** with the
same 30% lift reduction.

Read that carefully, because it is where a lot of the internet goes wrong. The paper
reports **drag** reductions. Drag is one loss among several — rolling resistance,
drivetrain losses and accessories do not shrink when the wake behind the cab does. A
5–6% drag cut does not hand you a 5–6% fuel saving.

The 30% lift reduction is the finding nobody quotes. Lift is the rear of the truck
getting light at speed, and less of it is a stability and towing argument that has
nothing to do with the pump.

### Why the three answers disagree

The tests were not sloppy, so the disagreement is in the trucks.

**Bed length.** A long bed with a big tailgate produces a large recirculating wake.
Closing it off changes more than closing off a short bed does. The Ram's 6-foot-4 bed
and the F-150's 8-foot bed have more wake to work with than the Maverick's and the
Tacoma's short boxes.

**What the cover does when it is open.** A hard roll-up stacks into a roughly 8-inch
bundle at the bulkhead even when open. A soft roll-up lies flatter. Neither is the
same shape as no cover at all, so "cover open" and "truck with no cover" are not
identical configurations — a point none of the three tests gets to fully isolate.

**Measurement resolution.** This is the big one. A 1.5 mpg difference on a 60-mile run
is a fraction of a gallon, and pump shutoff is not a precision instrument. The
Maverick test is the clearest illustration: the pump returned identical fuel numbers
and the dash returned a 1.8 mpg gap. When the two instruments disagree by that much,
the true difference is probably smaller than the number that gets quoted.

### The topper comparison, for scale

A related test on the same channel gives a sense of how small these margins get. A
2004 F-150 with the 4.6-liter V8 — 8-foot bed, about 4,900 feet of elevation, a
53.96-mile loop at a 70 mph cruise — ran **18.16 mpg bare** and **18.26 mpg with a
roughly 200 lb camper shell fitted.** One tenth of a mile per gallon. The shell
probably smoothed the airflow and probably cost the benefit back in weight, and the
two effects very nearly cancelled. Any tonneau claim in the low single digits should
be read against that.

### What a cover is worth for reasons you can count

Fuel is the weakest argument for a tonneau, and the one advertised hardest. The three
reasons that hold up regardless of what the pump says are weather, security and resale.
A bed that stays dry stays rust-free. A hard cover is a rigid barrier over the load,
and a soft one at least keeps things out of sight. And a cover transfers to the next
owner with the truck.

If you want a tonneau for those reasons, the fuel question stops mattering, and the
purchase should be decided on how you use the bed instead.

### Frequently asked questions

**Do tonneau covers improve gas mileage?**

Sometimes, by a small and hard-to-measure margin. Real highway tests returned +1.6 mpg,
a disputed 5%, and essentially nothing on three different trucks. The only primary
aerodynamic source reports 5–6% *drag* reduction from a bed cover, which is not the
same quantity as a 5–6% fuel saving.

**How much can I realistically expect?**

Budget a low-single-digit percentage at highway speed, if anything. The 200 lb camper
shell test is the reality check: 18.16 mpg bare against 18.26 mpg with a shell.

**Is a hard cover better than a soft one for economy?**

No. Fuel does not care whether the panel over the bed is vinyl or aluminium. The
choice between them is about security, and about how much room the open cover takes
up at the bulkhead.

### What this means on your own truck

If fuel is the reason, expect a gain your own before-and-after will struggle to detect,
and use the pump rather than the dash to try: same station both ways, GPS distance,
same route in both directions.

If weather, security or resale are the reason, the fuel number is a tiebreaker at most.

테스트가 엉성해서가 아니라 차가 달라서입니다.

**짐칸 길이.** 롱베드는 테일게이트 뒤에 큰 후류가 생기고, 숏베드는 그 양이 적습니다.

**측정 분해능.** 60마일 주행에서 1.5 mpg 차이는 갤런 단위로 몇 분의 일입니다. 주유 노즐이
자동으로 멈추는 지점은 정밀 계측기가 아닙니다. 매버릭 테스트가 이를 가장 선명하게
보여줍니다. pump는 같은 값, 대시는 1.8 mpg 차이였습니다.

### 캠퍼 셸과 비교하면

같은 채널의 2004 F-150 4.6L V8 테스트(8피트 베드, 53.96마일, 70mph 크루즈)는 셸 없이
**18.16 mpg**, 약 200파운드 셸을 얹고 **18.26 mpg**였습니다. 0.1 mpg 차이입니다. 공력 개선과
무게 증가가 거의 상쇄된 셈이고, 한 자릿수 초반의 토노 연비 주장은 이 숫자 옆에 놓고 읽어야
합니다.

### 연비 말고 셀 수 있는 이유

날씨, 보안, 잔존가치입니다. 짐칸이 마르면 부식이 줄고, 하드 커버는 짐을 가리는 물리적
장벽이 되며, 커버는 차와 함께 다음 주인에게 넘어갑니다. 이 셋이 목적이라면 연비 논쟁은
결정 요소가 아닙니다.

**토노 커버가 연비를 올리나요?**

## ES

**La misma pregunta se ha probado en vídeo al menos tres veces, y las tres respuestas no
coinciden.** Una Ram 1500 de 2022 ganó 1,6 mpg con la cubierta puesta. Una Ford Maverick
de 2022 salió un 5% mejor en el ordenador de viaje y exactamente igual en el surtidor.
Una Toyota Tacoma de 2026 subió y luego bajó a lo largo de tres tramos consecutivos.

Esa dispersión es la respuesta honesta, y es más útil que decir que las cubiertas ahorran
gasolina o que son un mito.

### Qué hizo cada prueba

| Prueba | Camioneta | Ruta | Con cubierta | Sin cubierta | Ganancia |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TFL, dic. 2022 | Ram 1500 2022, V6 3.6L | ~57 mi, 65 y 75 mph | 21,1 mpg | 19,5 mpg | **+1,6** |
| Daily Motor | Maverick 2022 2.0L | 60 mi, ~70 mph | 34,4 mpg* | 32,6 mpg* | **+5%** |
| Daily Motor | Tacoma TRD OR Hybrid 2026 | 3 × 50 mi, 71 mph | 22,5 y 23,0 | 23,5 | **~0** |

\*Las cifras de la Maverick son del ordenador de viaje. En el surtidor entraron 1,862 y
1,863 galones: el mismo número dos veces.

### La prueba de la Ram: el +1,6 mpg más limpio

The Fast Lane Truck recorrió unos 57 millas por GPS con una Ram 1500 Tradesman de 2022,
V6 Pentastar de 3,6 litros y 305 hp, a los límites de 65 y 75 mph. Con cubierta blanda
Mopar: **57,0 millas con 2,696 galones, 21,1 mpg.** Sin ella: **57 millas con 2,918
galones, 19,5 mpg.**

Dos detalles la hacen la más fiable. Al quitar la cubierta **la dejaron dentro de la
caja**, así que los 25–30 lb estuvieron presentes en ambas pasadas y la comparación
aísla la forma, no el peso. Y calcularon siempre con el surtidor, no con el tablero:
con razón, porque el ordenador marcó 21,9 mpg en la pasada sin cubierta frente a 19,5
reales.

### La prueba de la Maverick: el surtidor no vio cambio

Daily Motor hizo 60 millas con una Maverick XLT 2022 2.0L EcoBoost, unos 10.000 millas
en el odómetro y una Truxedo Sentry CT. Control de crucero a 72 mph de GPS, 35 psi en
frío, climatizador a 73 y 48°F ambiente.

Con la cubierta abierta: **60,8 millas con 1,862 galones, 32,6 mpg.** Cerrada: **60,8
millas con 1,863 galones, 32,6 mpg.** Una milésima de galón de diferencia.

El ordenador de viaje discrepó: 32,6 y 34,4, una brecha de 1,8 mpg que equivale al 5,5%
y que el presentador redondeó al 5%. Él mismo dijo que desconfiaba de dos repostajes tan
parecidos. El 5% sale del tablero, no del combustible que entró en el depósito.

### La prueba de la Tacoma: la respuesta se invirtió dos veces

El mismo canal probó después una Tacoma TRD Off-Road 2026 con el híbrido i-Force Max y
una BAK Revolver X4S, con tres tramos de 50 millas: cerrada, abierta, cerrada.

El resultado fue **22,5 cerrada, 23,5 abierta y 23,0 cerrada.** El tramo intermedio ganó
a los dos con la cubierta bajada.

El presentador explicó por qué no se fiaba de ese orden: el tercer tramo tuvo obras,
bajó a 65,1 mph de media frente a 67,1 y pilló dos chaparrones. También señaló que el
dueño había quitado el deflector frontal y que la caja y la cabina cortas dejan menos
estela que una caja larga.

### Lo que dice el túnel de viento

La fuente primaria real es más antigua y más estrecha de lo que sugieren los blogs. El
**trabajo SAE 881874**, de Maxwell, Jones y Jones (Texas Tech), presentado el **7 de
noviembre de 1988**, probó un alerón tras la cabina y una cubierta sobre la parte
trasera de la caja. Según el resumen, **ambos dieron reducciones de resistencia del 5%
al 6%**, y la cubierta sola **redujo la sustentación un 30%**. Juntos, en modelo a
escala, unos **17%** de reducción de resistencia.

Ahí está el error habitual: el trabajo informa de **resistencia aerodinámica**, no de
consumo. La resistencia es una pérdida entre varias; la rodadura y las pérdidas de
transmisión no bajan porque la estela se ordene. Un 5–6% menos de resistencia no es un
5–6% menos de consumo.

### Por qué discrepan las tres respuestas

**Longitud de caja.** Una caja larga genera una estela grande tras el portón; una corta,
mucho menos.

**Forma con la cubierta abierta.** Una enrollable rígida deja un bulto de unos 8 pulgadas
en el fondo. "Cubierta abierta" y "camioneta sin cubierta" no son la misma forma.

**Resolución de medida.** 1,5 mpg en 60 millas es una fracción de galón, y el corte
automático del surtidor no es un instrumento de precisión. La prueba de la Maverick lo
demuestra: el surtidor dio lo mismo y el tablero dio 1,8 mpg de diferencia.

### Comparación con una capota

Una prueba del mismo canal con una F-150 de 2004, V8 4.6L y caja de 8 pies dio **18,16
mpg sin capota** y **18,26 mpg con una capota de unos 200 lb** en un recorrido de 53,96
millas. Una décima. Cualquier cifra de un dígito bajo hay que leerla junto a esa.

### Para qué sirve una cubierta de verdad

Clima, seguridad y valor de reventa. Si ésas son las razones, la discusión sobre el
consumo deja de importar.
