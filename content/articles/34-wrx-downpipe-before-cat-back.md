# WRX Downpipe Before Cat-Back: The Order That Works, and the Part That Got Pulled

## EN

**On a turbo Subaru the downpipe is the first real power step, and the review data
says people buy it that way — the most-reviewed power part on the WRX is a catted
downpipe with 279 reviews. It is also the part the biggest name in Subaru tuning
has quietly stopped selling.**

That is the whole tension of this platform in one sentence, and it changes what
you should buy first. So this guide does two things: it tells you the mechanical
order, and it tells you what changed in the law and in the catalog, because
buying in the old order in 2026 means buying something you may not be able to
keep on the car.

### Why the downpipe is first

The turbo sits on the exhaust manifold and the downpipe bolts directly to its
outlet. Everything the engine exhales passes through that pipe before it reaches
the cat-back, the resonator, or the muffler. Restriction there costs more than
restriction anywhere downstream of it, because the gas is hotter, denser and
moving faster at that point.

MAPerformance says it plainly on their own product page: *"the factory 2015-2021
Subaru WRX downpipe is a huge bottleneck, limiting flow and robbing power."* That
is the manufacturer's claim for their own part, so treat it as a claim rather than
a measurement. But the physics is not controversial — a larger, mandrel-bent pipe
with a higher-flowing converter immediately behind the turbine does flow more than
the factory piece.

### What COBB did, and why it matters more than the review count

Here is the part that should change your plan.

COBB's product page for the 2015–2021 WRX NexGen Stage 2 Power Package contains
this sentence, in COBB's own words: *"NexGen Power Packages re-establish the
modification and upgrade paths for supported vehicles that have been impacted by
the removal of Downpipes. These packages provide similar performance and
excitement while maintaining legal emissions standards."*

Read that twice. The company that made its name tuning Subarus is describing a
world where **downpipes were removed from the upgrade path**, and their answer is
a package that gets the power back without one.

So what is in the Stage 2 package instead? Read off the same page:
Accessport V3, SF intake system, charge pipe kit, top-mount intercooler, and a
3-port boost control solenoid. No downpipe. The whole package is **$2,565.00,
discounted to $2,180.25**, and it carries **CARB EO D-660-172**.

And in COBB's own catalog, a search for "wrx downpipe" on 17 November 2026
returned **40 results, of which zero were named downpipe or J-pipe** — the hit
list was cat-backs and stage packages. Widening the search to plain "downpipe"
returned **67 results**, whose first entry was a Mitsubishi Evo X piece. COBB
still sells downpipes; it does not sell one for the WRX.

### The emissions picture, stated plainly

This is the section that decides the purchase, so it gets the numbers.

**COBB Accessport V3, AP3-SUB-004** (2015–2021 WRX/STI): **$770.00, discounted
to $654.50**. COBB's emissions block says *"50 State Legal"* and gives **CARB
Executive Order D-660-16 and D-660-43**.

**COBB Accessport V3, AP3-SUB-006** (2022–2026 WRX): also **$770.00, discounted
to $654.50**. Same "50 State Legal" statement, with **seven** EOs listed —
**D-660-125, D-660-153, D-660-179, D-660-193, D-660-225, D-660-257, D-660-317**.

**COBB Stage 1+ SF Power Package** (SUB0041W1P, 2015–2021 WRX): **$1,180.00,
discounted to $1,003.00**, 10 reviews, **EO D-660-109**.

Now the contrast. Every MAPerformance and Invidia downpipe listing checked for
this guide carried **no CARB text of any kind** — no EO number, no 50-state
claim, no California notice. Not "legal," not "not legal," simply absent.

**So the honest answer on this platform: a downpipe with no EO number is not
legal for street use in California and will fail a smog visual inspection. The
tune, from COBB, has EO numbers and is.** Those two facts point in opposite
directions, and no amount of wanting the downpipe changes them.

If you are in a state that inspects, the tune is legal and the downpipe is the
part you should think hardest about. Ask any seller for the EO number before you
buy. If the listing cannot produce one, the answer is that there isn't one.

### The order to buy things

**Tune first — and specifically the COBB one.** At $654.50 it is the cheapest
thing on this page and the only one with a CARB number you can read. It is also
the foundation every other purchase depends on: a downpipe on the factory
calibration is not a modification, it is a check-engine light waiting to happen.
Off-the-shelf maps for the parts below ship on the Accessport at no extra cost.

**Intake and intercooler second, if you want power legally.** This is exactly
what COBB's Stage 1+ and NexGen Stage 2 packages are built to do, and both carry
EOs — D-660-109 and D-660-172 respectively. **$1,003.00** gets you the Accessport
plus the SF intake; **$2,180.25** gets the full Stage 2. Expensive, and it is the
route that keeps the car legal.

**Downpipe third — and only with your eyes open.** Mechanically it is the correct
third step and the 279-review MAP part at $749.99 is the best-evidenced version of
it. It has no EO. If you live somewhere without an emissions inspection and you
accept that, buy it with a tune in the same order, not before.

**Cat-back last, and buy it for sound.** The Tomei at $1,161.00 with 201 reviews
is the standout. A cat-back sits behind the converter, so it does not touch
emissions equipment — but no listing here states its status either way, and
silence is not clearance. Buy it because you want to hear the car.

Worth knowing before you shop, because the 2.0 and the 2.5 are not the same
engine and the EPA data shows it. All figures read from fueleconomy.gov,
per-model pages, on 17 November 2026:

**The 2.5-litre STI is rated 4 mpg worse than the 2.0 WRX in every year both
existed**, and it lost a mile of city rating over that run — 17 in 2015 and 2017,
16 in 2020 and 2021. The 2022 car moved to 2.4 litres and landed at 22, one worse
than the 2.0 it replaced. Premium fuel is specified in every one of these rows.

That matters for a build because the STI's larger engine is also why its fitment
and its downpipe part numbers diverge from the WRX. MAP sells `WRX-4G-DPC-REV4`
for the WRX and `STI-4G-DPC-REV2` for the STI — different pipes, $749.99 and
$699.99 respectively, and the STI part covers 2008 through 2021, an unusually long
run. Buying the wrong one is a fitment return, not a tuning problem.

### The cheap part nobody mentions

There is one $62 item in this sweep that solves the most common problem with this
whole plan, and it is worth naming: **GrimmSpeed's OEM downpipe to 3-inch cat-back
adapter, $62.00, 66 reviews.** The factory downpipe and most aftermarket cat-backs
do not share a flange size. If you buy the cat-back first — or buy a downpipe and
keep the stock system — an adapter is what makes the two ends meet.

66 reviews on a $62 adapter is a lot of people solving the same geometry problem.
Read that as evidence for the ordering argument: **plenty of owners end up with a
downpipe and a cat-back that do not bolt to each other**, which only happens if
you buy them without planning the whole path.

### What this guide is not saying

The downpipe is still the best power-per-dollar hard part on a turbo Subaru. This
guide is not arguing otherwise, and the review counts show the market agrees with
that.

What changed is that in 2026 you cannot treat "downpipe then cat-back" as a
complete answer, because the emissions side of it is no longer a footnote. COBB
adjusted its catalog and wrote the reason on its own product pages. That is worth
more attention than any single dyno claim on a listing.
