# LED Headlights: The Street Legal Field Is Published, the Lumens Are Not

## EN

**Three LED headlight products, from $148.99 to $464.75, and the one that says
"Street Legal: Yes" is the one that publishes no lumen figure, no DOT marking and
no certification at all.** The most expensive of the three states DOT-approved in
writing and fills in the certification fields one way or the other, even when the
answer is No.

On headlights, the number people shop by — brightness — is the number least
often published. What is published, if you know where to look, is whether the
manufacturer can name a standard.

**Every figure below was read from the product spec block on 19 November 2026.**

### Three products, the fields that are actually filled in

| Product | Price | Type | Colour temp | Street legal | DOT | Quantity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PIAA LEH172E | $148.99 | Bulb kit, H8/H9/H11/H16 | 6600K | **Yes** | **not stated** | Pair |
| Oracle 5769-123 | $324.79 | 7 in. sealed LED | not published | not stated | **not stated** | **Sold individually** |
| J.W. Speaker 8700 Evo 2 | $464.75 | 7 in. sealed LED | not published | **DOT-approved** | **Yes** | Pair |

The spread in what is disclosed is wider than the spread in price.

### What "Street Legal: Yes" is and is not

The PIAA kit's spec row reads **Street Legal: Yes**, and its description calls
the bulbs "premium street-legal replacements." That is a claim in the product
description, made by the manufacturer, and it is worth taking seriously — it is
not nothing.

But read what else is on that page:

- No **lumens** field.
- No **candlepower** field.
- No **DOT** field.
- No **SAE** field.

A search of the page text for each of those four terms returned absent every
time. So "Street Legal: Yes" is standing alone, with no cited standard behind it
on the listing.

That is not an accusation of dishonesty. LED replacement bulbs occupy a grey
area precisely because the rules were written around halogen assemblies, and a
manufacturer can reasonably describe a product as street legal while publishing
no lumen figure at all. The point is narrower and more useful: **on this page,
the word is all you get, and the word is not checkable.**

### The product that names its standard

The **J.W. Speaker 8700 Evo 2, $464.75**, does the opposite. Its notes read, in
full: *"Vehicles equipped with factory LED headlights will require the J.W.
Speaker mounting rings. Lights feature black trim. DOT-approved."*

Its spec block also carries two fields the other two listings also carry, and
this is the detail worth slowing down for:

- **CAPA Certified: No**
- **NSF Certified: No**

A $464.75 pair, described as DOT-approved, reporting No on both certification
fields. That looks contradictory and is not: the two fields answer a different
question from the DOT note. CAPA and NSF are third-party aftermarket-part quality
programmes; a DOT marking is about the lamp meeting a federal lighting standard.
A lamp can carry the DOT marking and hold neither third-party certification.

The value here is that the page publishes all three answers. You can see the
standard it claims, and you can see which third-party certifications it does not
hold. **Compare that with a page that publishes only the word "legal."**

### What the price difference actually buys

The two sealed 7-inch units are the cleaner comparison, because both replace a
whole 7 in. lamp rather than a bulb inside an existing housing:

- **Oracle 5769-123, $324.79**, sold **individually**, with a white Halo/DRL
  included, and no DOT claim anywhere on the page.
- **J.W. Speaker 8700 Evo 2, $464.75**, sold **as a pair**, no Halo/DRL, and
  DOT-approved.

Read the quantity field before the price field, always. **The Oracle is $324.79
for one; the J.W. Speaker is $464.75 for two.** Per lamp, that is $324.79 against
$232.38 — so the DOT-approved pair is the cheaper option per unit by **$92.41**,
and it is the only one of the three that states a standard.

That inversion disappears entirely if you compare sticker prices without reading
the quantity line, which is how most of these are bought.

### Halos, DRLs and what they add

The Oracle carries **Halo/DRL Included: Yes, Halo Color: White**, and its notes
add that it comes with SwitchBack amber turn signals. The J.W. Speaker reads
**Halo/DRL Included: No** and **Turn Signal Included: No**.

If a halo is the reason you are buying, that is a real difference and the Oracle
is the only one of the three that has it. If it is not, you are paying for a
feature you did not want — and on a per-lamp basis, more than the DOT-approved
pair.

### Colour temperature is not brightness

The PIAA publishes **6600K**, and that number gets read as a brightness spec far
more often than it should.

Kelvin measures the **colour** of the light, not how much of it there is. 6600K
is on the blue side of white — cooler than daylight, and noticeably cooler than
a halogen, which typically sits well below that. It changes how the light looks
and how it renders colour at night. It says nothing about how far down the road
you can see.

No product in this article publishes a lumen figure. That means brightness is not
comparable across these three from the listings alone, and any table that ranks
them by it would be inventing the numbers.

### Why the bulb kit and the sealed unit are different purchases

The PIAA is a **bulb kit** — H8/H9/H11/H16 — that goes into a housing you already
have. At **18.0 watts** low beam and described as controller-free, it is the
plug-and-play path.

The Oracle and J.W. Speaker are complete **7.000 in. sealed lamps** with their own
lens and housing. They replace the whole assembly, which is why they cost what
they cost and why their fitment is measured in lamp diameter rather than bulb
socket.

Buying the wrong type is the most common and most expensive mistake here: a bulb
kit cannot fix a worn housing, and a sealed lamp will not fit a vehicle whose
headlight is not a 7 in. round unit to begin with. The J.W. Speaker notes add one
more layer — factory-LED vehicles need **mounting rings** as a separate part.

### Reading a headlight listing in the right order

1. **Type.** Bulb kit or sealed lamp, and the socket or diameter.
2. **Quantity.** Sold individually or as a pair. This changes the real price by
   a factor of two.
3. **DOT, CAPA, NSF.** Three separate fields. Read all three; No is an answer.
4. **Lumens or candlepower.** Often absent — and if it is absent, that is
   information rather than a gap to fill in.
5. **Colour temperature.** A look, not an output.
6. **Price**, after the first two.

### Does any of this touch California emissions law?

No. Headlights are not emissions equipment, there is no CARB Executive Order to
check, and lighting has no bearing on a smog inspection.

Lighting legality in California is a vehicle equipment question, and it is a
different rulebook from the one this site checks for intakes, exhausts and
tuners. Worth not confusing the two.

### What I could not verify

- **Lumen output for any of the three.** None publishes a figure, so none is
  quoted. The brightness ranking people want from an article like this cannot be
  honestly built from these listings.
- **Whether the PIAA kit is legal in any specific state.** It says Street Legal:
  Yes. I did not find a standard, a test report or a DOT marking behind that
  statement on the page, and I am not going to assert one on the manufacturer's
  behalf.
- **Actual beam pattern or cutoff** for any of the three. That is what
  determines glare to oncoming traffic, and no listing publishes it.
- **Fitment beyond socket and diameter.** Application tables load separately and
  were not read. Confirm by year, make, model and trim.
- **Lifespan in hours.** Descriptions mention longer service life without
  publishing a number.

**J.W. Speaker 8700 Evo 2, $464.75**는 반대입니다. 노트에 *"DOT-approved"*라고

모순처럼 보이지만 아닙니다. CAPA와 NSF는 애프터마켓 부품 품질에 관한 제3자

표시가 있으면서 두 인증이 모두 없을 수 있습니다.

중요한 것은 이 페이지가 세 가지 답을 모두 공개한다는 점입니다.

### Qué significa «Legal: Sí»

La ficha del PIAA dice **Legal: Sí** y su descripción lo llama reemplazo
«street-legal». No es poca cosa.

Pero en esa misma página no hay campo de **lúmenes**, ni de **intensidad
lumínica**, ni de **DOT**, ni de **SAE**. Busqué las cuatro palabras y no aparecen.
La afirmación queda sola, sin norma citada.

No es una acusación: las bombillas LED de reemplazo ocupan un terreno gris porque
las normas se escribieron para conjuntos halógenos. El punto es más concreto: en
esa página la palabra es todo lo que hay, y no se puede comprobar.

### El producto que nombra su norma

El **J.W. Speaker 8700 Evo 2, $464,75**, hace lo contrario. Sus notas dicen
*«DOT-approved»* y la ficha añade **CAPA: No** y **NSF: No**.

Parece contradictorio y no lo es: CAPA y NSF son programas externos de calidad de
piezas de posventa, mientras que una marca DOT se refiere a que la lámpara cumple
una norma federal de iluminación. Un faro puede llevar la marca DOT sin tener
ninguna de las dos certificaciones.

### Qué compra la diferencia de precio

**El Oracle cuesta $324,79 por una unidad; el J.W. Speaker, $464,75 por dos.** Por
faro son $324,79 frente a $232,38: el par con aprobación DOT es **$92,41 más
barato por unidad**, y es el único que declara una norma. Esa inversión desaparece
si se comparan las etiquetas sin leer la cantidad.

### Temperatura de color y California

Los 6600K miden color, no cantidad de luz. Ninguno de los tres publica lúmenes, así
que no se puede clasificar su brillo con la información de las fichas.

Los faros no son equipo de emisiones: no hay Executive Order de CARB y no afectan
a la inspección de humos. La legalidad de la iluminación es una cuestión de equipo
del vehículo, y es un reglamento distinto del que este sitio consulta para
admisiones, escapes y programadores.

### Lo que no pude verificar

No se publican lúmenes, patrón de haz ni vida útil en horas. No leí las tablas de
aplicación: confirme el montaje por año, marca, modelo y versión.
