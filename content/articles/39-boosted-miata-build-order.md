# Boosted Miata Build Order: The Parts That Decide Whether It Survives

## EN

**Every turbo Miata video is about the turbo. The parts that decide whether the
car is still running in two years are the four that never appear in the thumbnail
— the ECU, the fuel pump, the clutch and the radiator.** This is the order those
four go in, priced from live listings read on 18 November 2026.

There is one thing worth stating before any of it, because it changes how you
shop. The retailer these prices come from splits the Miata catalog cleanly and
inconveniently in half. Every standalone ECU on it fits a **1990–2005 NA or NB**.
Both forced-induction kits on it fit a **2016+ ND**. There is no single model
year where you can buy the computer and the boost from the same place.

### What the catalog actually contains

That split is not a glitch in the search. The MegaSquirt plug-and-play units are
listed in five year bands covering 1990 through 2005, and the Haltech adaptor
harness kits in two. The two supercharger kits are listed for the ND only.

So a boosted Miata project on this platform is two purchases from two kinds of
seller. The computer, the fuel system, the clutch and the cooling come from a
parts catalog. The turbocharger, the manifold and the downpipe come from a Miata
specialist who builds them for that car. Neither half is optional and neither half
is the fun one to film.

The rest of this guide prices the half that is in the catalog.

the engine from going lean at the top of a pull, which is the failure that holes
a piston. Buy it before the turbo, not after.

For the NA and NB cars, where the standalones live, the turbocharger comes from a
Miata specialist rather than from this catalog. That is the one purchase in the
build you cannot price from a listing page, and the one you should price last —
after the ECU, the pump, the clutch and the radiator, because those four set the
boost ceiling you can safely run.

### The order to buy things

**1. The ECU.** Everything else is chosen to match it. The Gen2 at $829.99 if
your car is 1994–2000, the MS3Pro at $1,299.99 if it is 2001–2005.

**2. The wideband.** $229.99 for the MTX-L+ with a display. You cannot tune
without a number to look at.

**3. The fuel pump.** $146.30 for the ISR 340 lph. Before the boost, not after.

**4. The clutch.** $525.00 for the Competition Stage 2 on a 1.8, and $406.00 for
the flywheel while the gearbox is already out.

**5. The radiator and thermostat.** $299.95 and $68.95. The most-reviewed part
in the catalog, and the cheapest of the four options.

**6. The boost.** Last, priced from a specialist, sized to whatever the first
five will support.

That is **$1,871.24** in parts before the turbocharger — ECU, pump, clutch and
radiator, at $899.99 + $146.30 + $525.00 + $299.95 on a 1994–1995 car. Every one
of those four fits the same model year, which is not true of every combination on
this page. Set that against the $5,310.00 HKS kit for an ND and it is clear the
supporting parts are the cheap half of a boosted build. They are also the half
that determines whether the expensive half survives.

### Is any of this legal in California?

Every part in this guide was checked for a CARB Executive Order number on
18 November 2026. **None of them carries one.** The searches returned no EO
number on any ECU, any fuel pump, any clutch, any radiator or either supercharger
kit — the only matches for the letters "CARB" across these listings were the
words "**Carb**on Fiber" and "**carb**ureted" inside unrelated product copy.

Without an EO number, a standalone ECU and a forced-induction kit are **not legal
for street use in California**, and they fail smog visual inspection even when
the car reads clean at the tailpipe. A replacement radiator, a clutch and a fuel
pump of the same specification are service parts and are a different question;
the ECU and the boost are the two that change the emissions equipment. Ask the
seller for an EO number in writing before you buy either, and if there is not
one, the car is not a California street car any more.
