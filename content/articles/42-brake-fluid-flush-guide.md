# Brake Fluid: The Wet Boiling Point Is the Only Number That Matters

## EN

**Brake fluid is the only part of the brake system that wears out on a clock
instead of on mileage.** A pad wears when you use it. Fluid degrades while the
car sits in the driveway, because glycol-based brake fluid absorbs water out of
the air, and water lowers the temperature at which the fluid turns to gas.

That single fact reorganises the whole buying decision, and it is why the number
printed in the biggest type on the bottle is usually the wrong one.

### What the law actually requires

Brake fluid sold in the US is governed by FMVSS 116, and the boiling-point
requirement is not a marketing claim — it is a floor. From the regulation itself:

> **DOT 4: 230 °C. (446 °F)** equilibrium reflux boiling point
> **DOT 4: 155 °C. (311 °F)** wet boiling point

Both figures come from 49 CFR 571.116. The first is the fluid as it comes out of
a sealed container. The second is the fluid after it has been deliberately
exposed to moisture, and it is roughly **135°F lower**.

That gap is the entire subject of this article.

### Dry versus wet, and which one your car is at

A sealed bottle is dry. Your brake system is not. Every time you open the
reservoir, every time a rubber seal breathes, every time the fluid heats and
cools, moisture gets in. Brake fluid is hygroscopic — it pulls water in by
design, which is better than letting that water pool as droplets, but it means
the fluid in a two-year-old car is somewhere between dry and wet, drifting toward
wet the entire time.

So the dry number describes a bottle. **The wet number describes your brakes.**

This is why a car that stopped fine last summer can fade a pedal on a mountain
descent this summer without anything having broken. The fluid did not fail. It
absorbed water and lost boiling margin, exactly as specified.

### The cleanest comparison in the whole catalog

Here is the pairing worth studying, because it removes every confound.

The **Summit Racing SUM-943020 is $14.99 for 16.90 oz.** The **Motul 600 is
$23.99 for 500 ml.** Those are not different volumes — 16.90 US fluid ounces is
499.8 ml. **Same bottle size, to within a rounding error.**

And Summit publishes the boiling points in its own listing:

- **Summit Racing DOT 4: 572°F dry, 383°F wet**
- **Motul 600: 594°F dry, 399°F wet**

So for **$9 more**, Motul gives you **22°F more dry boiling point** and **16°F
more wet boiling point**.

Twenty-two degrees of dry margin is real but academic — both fluids are far above
the 446°F legal floor when new. Sixteen degrees of *wet* margin is the one that
does work, because wet is the state your car is drifting toward. If you are
boiling fluid on a track day, 16°F is meaningful. If you are commuting, the
Summit bottle already carries 72°F of margin over the 311°F floor — with under a quarter
of that margin being the difference between it and the premium bottle.

Note the shape of the number: **both fluids lose well over 180°F going from dry
to wet.** 572 to 383 is a 189°F drop; 594 to 399 is a 195°F drop. The premium
fluid does not resist moisture better in any dramatic sense. It starts higher and
lands higher, by almost exactly the same amount it started ahead.

### The one-liter Brembo, and why volume changes the math

The **Brembo L04210 is $14.99 for a full liter** — 33.8 ounces, twice the Motul's
volume, at $0.44 an ounce. That is within two cents per ounce of the cheapest

A typical flush consumes well under a liter on most passenger vehicles. If a

costs more than buying the big one once.

The Brembo is also a **low-viscosity** DOT 4 formulation, which is a real
distinction on vehicles with ABS and stability control — those systems have small
orifices and a lower-viscosity fluid moves through them faster. Brembo publishes
that it is low viscosity in the product name itself, which is more than most
listings tell you.

### DOT 4, DOT 3, DOT 5.1 — and the one that must not be mixed

**DOT 3** has a lower dry boiling point than DOT 4. **DOT 4** is the common
upgrade and is what everything on this page is. **DOT 5.1** is higher still and is
compatible with DOT 3 and DOT 4 systems — it is a glycol fluid despite the
number.

**DOT 5 is silicone and is not compatible with any of them.** It must never be
mixed into a DOT 3, 4 or 5.1 system. The naming is the trap: DOT 5.1 sounds newer
and better than DOT 5, and the two are chemically unrelated.

If you do not know what is in the car now, do not guess at it — a flush that
mixes chemistries is worse than old fluid.

### When to change it

The commonly published street interval is **every two years**, and the reason is
the absorption clock rather than mileage. A car that covers 3,000 miles a year
still pulls moisture into the reservoir for two full years.

Track use is a different schedule entirely — many owners change before an event
rather than on a calendar, because a wet fluid's margin disappears fast under
repeated heavy braking.

**Color is not a reliable test.** Fresh DOT 4 is amber and darkens with age, but
the relationship between color and water content is loose enough that a fluid can
look acceptable and test badly. A tester, or the two-year clock, beats an eyeball.

### The procedure, and where DIY goes wrong

**1. Find the bleeder screws** before you buy anything. Bleeding order follows
their location, and the sequence on a truck with a load-proportioning valve is
not the same as on a small car. That sequence comes from the service manual for
your vehicle, not from a generic rule.

**2. Never let the reservoir run dry.** Air pulled into the master cylinder means
bench-bleeding it, which turns a one-hour job into an afternoon. Top up after
every caliper.

**3. Do not pump the pedal to the floor** on an older vehicle. The pedal travels
further than it does in normal use and can push the master cylinder seal through
corrosion it normally never touches — a leak created by the bleeding itself. Firm
half-travel strokes are the safer technique.

**4. Fresh fluid is clear-ish and moves easily.** If what comes out of the bleeder
is dark and thick, keep going; you are still flushing the old fluid out.

**5. Dispose of the old fluid properly.** It is toxic and it is not something to
pour down a drain.

### Is any of this a CARB issue in California?

No. Brake fluid is not emissions equipment, there is no Executive Order to look
for, and a fluid change has no effect on a smog inspection.

The relevant regulation is federal, not California-specific: FMVSS 116 sets the
boiling-point floors quoted above, and it applies in every state.
