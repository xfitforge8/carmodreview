### What is actually holding the rotor on

Three things, and only one of them is a fastener.

The **wheel clamps the rotor to the hub**. That is the real retention — the
lugnuts pull the wheel face against the rotor hat and the friction between those
surfaces is what resists rotation. Once the wheel is off, that force is gone.

The **retaining screw** locates the rotor so the wheel holes line up. It carries
almost no load in service. It exists so the rotor does not fall off on the
assembly line and does not rotate out of alignment while you fit a wheel.

The **hub face** is where the problem lives. Two bare steel surfaces, exposed to
spray, held together for years, rust into one piece. That rust is what welds a
rotor to a hub, and no amount of hitting the friction surface addresses it.

That distinction is the whole diagnosis. A rotor that will not come off is
almost never held by the screw. It is held by rust at the hub interface, and the
screw is just the first thing in the way.

### The screw is the first obstacle, not the cause

The retaining screw is a small cross-head or Torx fastener, and on a truck that
has seen winters it is often already rounded before anyone touches it. There are
two legitimate ways to deal with it and one that ruins a hub.

| Approach | What it costs | When it is right |
|---|---|---|
| Impact driver, axial load | The tool you already own | Screw head is intact |
| Drill the head off | One drill bit | Head is rounded, rotor is being replaced |
| Heat and extractor | A torch and a set | Last resort, and only on the screw |

**Drilling the head off is not a failure.** The screw holds nothing once the
wheel is on. A rotor that is being replaced does not need its screw to survive,
and a drilled screw leaves the threaded stub in the hub, which comes out easily
once the rotor is off and there is nothing clamping it. The videos that show
this — the 2.4-million-view one in particular — are not teaching a trick. They
are teaching that the screw is expendable.

The mistake worth naming is attacking the screw with a hammer and a punch while
the rotor is still on. That deforms the rotor hat, and if you are reusing the
rotor, you have just made a new problem.

### Getting it off once the screw is out

With the caliper bracket off and the screw gone, the rotor is held only by rust
at the hub face. The sequence that works:

**1. Penetrating oil at the hub interface, not the wheel face.** The joint is
between the rotor hat and the hub flange, on the inboard side. Spraying the
braking surface does nothing.

**2. Thread a bolt into the removal hole if the rotor has one.** Many truck
rotors are drilled with two small threaded jacking holes between the studs.
Two bolts driven evenly push the rotor off the hub with no impact at all. Check
for these before anything else — a rotor that has them does not need to be hit.

**3. Strike the hat, not the friction ring.** A dead-blow against the flat of
the hat around the stud circle breaks the rust bond. Hitting the friction
surface warps it.

**4. If it still will not move, go back to the interface.** A chisel between
the rotor hat and the hub flange, working around the stud circle, separates the
two surfaces. This is where most stuck rotors finally give.

What is not on that list is heating the rotor until it glows. That is how a hub
bearing gets destroyed on a truck that is supposed to drive home.

### Is any of this legal in California?

Rotors, pads and retaining bolts are replacement service parts, and none of them
changes the emissions equipment on the vehicle. There is no CARB Executive Order
question here in the way there is with an intake or a tune.

What California does regulate is brake friction material content, under the
state's copper limitation for brake pads sold in the state. Both pads here are
sold by a national retailer for road use and neither listing carries any
restriction language — but if you are buying for a California-registered vehicle
and the listing does not state the friction material compliance, that is the
question to ask the seller, not one this page can answer for you.

### Qué retiene el disco en realidad

**La rueda sujeta el disco contra el buje.** Esa es la retención real: las tuercas
aprietan la cara de la rueda contra la campana y la fricción entre ambas superficies
resiste el giro. Sin rueda, esa fuerza desaparece.

**El tornillo de retención solo centra.** En servicio casi no soporta carga.

**La cara del buje es el problema.** Dos superficies de acero desnudo, expuestas años
a la lluvia y la sal, se convierten en una sola pieza. Golpear la superficie de frenado
no toca ese óxido.

### El tornillo es el primer obstáculo, no la causa

**Rebajar la cabeza con una broca no es un fallo.** Con la rueda puesta, ese tornillo
no sujeta nada. Una vez fuera el disco, el resto roscado sale con facilidad.

Antes de golpear nada, comprueba si el disco tiene **dos agujeros roscados de expulsión**
entre los espárragos. Muchos discos de camioneta los llevan: dos pernos apretados por
igual lo sacan sin impacto.
